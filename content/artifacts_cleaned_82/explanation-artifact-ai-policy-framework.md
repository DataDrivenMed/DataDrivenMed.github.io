## **Explanation Artifact: LSUHSC School of Medicine AI Policy Framework**

**What is this** The pre-final School of Medicine AI policy draft and accompanying white paper form a targeted educational governance framework for clinical training. Unlike the institution's broad IT compliance rulebook, the SOM draft policy specifically frames what medical residents and students may be allowed to do after leadership review and institutional alignment - such as enforcing a "sequence rule" that requires independent clinical assessment before any AI query. The White Paper acts as a strategic briefing document, providing program directors with the stark regulatory context needed to implement immediate program-level AI policies and PHI acknowledgments while the broader institution catches up.

**Why this approach** I deliberately built a separate School of Medicine policy rather than amending the institutional IT framework because clinical training rules belong under the Dean's academic authority, not the CIO's compliance umbrella. Attempting to merge them would have diluted crucial educational standards into unrecognizable bureaucratic language. While I used AI to rapidly synthesize the federal-state regulatory landscape and structure the compliance matrix, I had to repeatedly override its assumptions about organizational hierarchy. The AI defaulted to treating LSUHSC as a single, monolithic context, failing to understand that a resident rotating at an affiliate hospital is subject to competing policies - requiring me to manually architect and defend the "stricter-standard" principle.

**What would break** The most structurally fragile component is the multi-hospital dependency; if an affiliate hospital natively integrates an ambient AI scribe into their EHR workflows by default, the SOM's policy becomes practically meaningless against the social friction of disrupting a live clinical environment. Furthermore, the human-dependent "sequence rule" is an educational luxury designed for daytime rounds that will completely evaporate at 3 AM in the ICU when exhausted attendings prioritize documentation speed over teaching. I also know that provisions around personal device use and academic integrity are inherently unenforceable without fundamental changes to how faculty design assessments. The policy establishes the liability standard, but it cannot singlehandedly control human behavior in unstructured, high-stress moments.

**What I learned** I started assuming institutional governance was a top-down compliance problem, but I discovered it is actually a continuous negotiation between incompatible authority structures: the institution owns the tools, the Dean owns the education, and the affiliate hospitals own the clinical workflows. Throughout the process, AI continually suggested naive oversight mechanisms - like quarterly reporting - that falsely assumed the School of Medicine had surveillance authority inside an independent hospital's walls. If I had to rebuild this tomorrow, I wouldn't begin by auditing documents; I would start by

putting the affiliate CMIOs and program directors in a room to map out exactly where their operational jurisdictions collide. I learned the hard way that policy documents cannot resolve overlapping structural gaps - only active, ongoing relationships can.
