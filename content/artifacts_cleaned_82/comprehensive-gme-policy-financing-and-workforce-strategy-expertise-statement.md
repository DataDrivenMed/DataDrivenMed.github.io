# GME Policy, Financing & Workforce Strategy: Comprehensive Expertise Statement

## The Core Mandate

I manage graduate medical education strategy across LSU Health New Orleans—70+ residency and fellowship programs across three competing health systems (LCMC Health, OLOL, and Ochsner). The work sits at the intersection of three things: the mechanics of how CMS funds physician training, the workforce math that determines whether we're solving regional shortages or just perpetuating them, and the institutional politics of allocating scarce training slots when your health systems are competing for the same market.

Most GME leaders specialize in one. I work at the intersection because that's where decisions actually get made.

---

## Medicare GME Financing: The Technical Reality

The financial architecture of GME hasn't fundamentally changed since 1997, but the rules have become Byzantine—and the pressure to change them is building fast. Here's what matters:

**Direct Graduate Medical Education (DGME):** This is the per-resident allowance. CMS calculates it from your institution's historical costs, adjusted annually for inflation and wage index. The formula is straightforward until you look closer:
- FTE counting rules create incentives you don't expect. A resident rotating at an off-site clinic counts differently than one at the main hospital. An advanced practice provider in a training program creates accounting complexities most GME offices don't fully understand.
- The wage index adjustment hits us harder. Our Louisiana wage index depresses DGME payments compared to Northeast and West Coast programs training the same specialties. Over 10 residents over a decade, that's real money lost.
- Specialty mix is invisible leverage. Surgery and pediatrics have different historical cost bases. The same resident in different specialties generates different payments. If you understand this, you can make the case for why expanding one program versus another changes your total funding.

**Indirect Medical Education (IME):** This is the hospital adjustment—CMS's way of saying teaching hospitals cost more to run. It's calculated as a percentage of operating costs based on resident-to-bed ratio, but there's a catch:
- The payment caps at 7.5% of total operating costs, and the cap applies at the hospital level, not the program level.
- Run three competing systems, and that cap becomes zero-sum. If LCMC captures more IME funding, OLOL loses it. Managing that requires forecasting bed utilization, patient volume, and resident census all at once.
- The DGME-IME interaction creates leverage you can use. Concentrating residents in high-cost specialties at high-volume hospitals maximizes total CMS funding. But that may contradict your workforce strategy or teaching mission. So the choice isn't obvious.

**The 2020 Redistributed Slots:** Congress allocated 1,000 new residency positions in 2020. The specifics matter:
- Half went to rural tracks, half to shortage specialties.
- To get them, you had to commit to specific things: geographic diversity, primary care focus, training in community settings.
- We captured slots. That success required proving we had pipeline capacity and real demand. But it locked us into those commitments for a decade. That's a strategic bet I'm managing now.

**Medicaid and State-Level Funding:** CMS sets the floor. States supplement. Louisiana funds GME through:
- DSH adjustments that vary by institution and payer mix.
- State supplemental payments negotiated annually.
- GME add-ons for high-need specialties like family medicine and psychiatry.

The problem: These streams are politically fragile. A budget cut can trigger a 5% loss. I model three scenarios annually because the difference cascades through everything.

**The Shift Toward Performance-Based Reimbursement:** CMS is testing models where funding ties to outcomes—graduation rates, board pass rates, where residents practice, who they serve. These pilots are the real future because they change how you allocate resources. A program that trains residents who stay in Louisiana and work in shortage areas will be worth more than one that trains specialists bound for coastal metros.

## Managing Health System Conflict: The Three-System Reality

LCMC and OLOL compete directly in Louisiana. Both have residency programs. Both want to grow. I don't choose winners, but the financial architecture forces choices.

**Shared Slots and Allocation:**
- We have 12 internal medicine positions split: LCMC has 8, OLOL has 4. That ratio reflects history, not current market reality.
- LCMC's patient volume supports their allocation. OLOL's market share has shifted, but their GME funding hasn't.
- Reallocating slots to match actual capacity means OLOL loses revenue. Not reallocating means we're training residents in a declining market.
- My job is to present the numbers: What does each system's patient volume forecast look like? What's the ROI per resident trained in each location? What does the workforce look like in 3 years if we don't change?

**Managing the Implicit Subsidy:**
- LCMC's flagship hospital has higher volume and lower cost per resident. OLOL's teaching sites have different constraints.
- DGME payments are institution-specific, so this matters. Higher-cost training at lower-volume sites generates lower return on the same resident.
- I've built a model that shows: Given three market scenarios, what's the optimal allocation to maintain financial sustainability at both systems while preserving training quality?

**Faculty and Simulation Infrastructure:**
- Both systems want to keep faculty depth. Concentrating residents at one site creates redundancy at the other.
- Splitting training preserves institutional capability and market presence, but costs more to run.
- The solution uses simulation and off-site rotations strategically. A resident can be based at OLOL but rotate through LCMC's specialized services without changing how they count toward FTE census. That requires knowing the rules.

## Workforce Forecasting: From Data to Strategic Decisions

I track three datasets simultaneously:

**Graduate Destination Analysis:**
- Where do our residents practice 3, 5, and 10 years after graduation?
- Are we solving Louisiana's shortage in family medicine and psychiatry, or are we training cardiologists who leave?

I match graduation cohort data against physician registry data (NPI, board licensing, AMA masterfile). The lag is about 2 years, but the pattern becomes clear.

Example: Our internal medicine residents from 2021 stayed in Louisiana at 60%. That cohort had 85% with at least one rural rotation. The 2022 cohort had 72% rural rotations. I'm forecasting the 2025 cohort will see Louisiana retention drop to 52%. That tells me we need to either expand rural curriculum now or accept that we're training for external markets.

**Specialty Shortage Forecasting:**
- Louisiana has documented shortfalls in family medicine and psychiatry. But the numbers change based on practicing physician age, out-migration, population growth, and Medicaid enrollment.
- I model three scenarios for each specialty: baseline (trends continue), optimistic (funding improves, physicians attracted), pessimistic (economic downturn, reduced pressure).
- The output drives real decisions: Do we create two new family medicine positions or expand psychiatry? That depends on whether Louisiana will fund primary care or whether we hit capacity. I show leadership the ROI in each case.

**Program Viability and Right-Sizing:**
- I recommended closing a pediatric subspecialty fellowship that was training residents with no chance of employment in Louisiana. That freed $400K in annual DGME funding to redirect to psychiatry.
- I recommended expanding a hospitalist track based on modeling showing rising demand as hospital census grows in our area.
- These recommendations come with full financial modeling: DGME impact, IME adjustment, Medicaid supplemental changes, faculty costs, recruitment impact on the health system.

**Pipeline Integration with UME:**
- Our medical school produces 200 graduates annually. Not all stay in our residencies.
- I analyze the loss rate. If we're losing 40% of strong family medicine candidates to external programs, that's a problem. If we're losing dermatology candidates and dermatology isn't a shortage specialty, that's fine.
- I track specialty choice patterns: If 15% of our med students pursue dermatology, but Louisiana has no shortage in derm, those people will leave. If 8% pursue family medicine, but we need 12%, we have a pipeline gap.
- I work with medical school leadership on this because curriculum changes targeting family medicine interest can shift those percentages 2-3 points, which cascades through our residency system.

## The Integration: How This Actually Works

A real example from 2024:

**The Question:** OLOL wanted to expand their psychiatry program by 4 positions. LCMC already has psychiatry positions. Should we expand OLOL or concentrate at LCMC?

**The Data:**
- Louisiana has a severe psychiatry shortage. Practicing psychiatrists have a median age of 58. Over 5 years, retirements will create 40+ open positions statewide.
- Our three systems combined produce 8 psychiatry residents annually. Even if 90% stay in Louisiana, we're replacing 7 of 40 vacancies.
- OLOL's sites have capacity: 80+ psychiatric beds with high volume. Training more residents is operationally feasible.
- DGME funding: 4 new psychiatry positions at OLOL generates roughly $320K annually (based on psychiatry's cost base adjusted for OLOL's wage index). IME impact is minimal because OLOL's bed ratio doesn't change much.
- LCMC capacity: Their program is at 8 positions. Adding more requires facility expansion or off-site training, which increases costs and reduces quality.
- Workforce outcome: Based on prior cohorts, residents trained at OLOL stay in Louisiana at higher rates than LCMC residents, who have higher external fellowship placement rates.

**The Decision:** Expand at OLOL, 4 positions, with guardrails:
- All residents rotate through LCMC's academic center for 2 months to preserve educational quality.
- The program commits to rural rotations (targeting state supplemental Medicaid funding).
- 3-year review gates tied to grad outcomes (board pass rate, Louisiana practice placement, patient volume sustainability).

**The Impact:** +$320K DGME, potential +$100-150K Medicaid supplemental funding, net +$420-470K annually. Over 10 years, potentially 3-4 additional psychiatrists staying in Louisiana.

That decision requires fluency in funding mechanics, health system capacity, workforce forecasting, and credibility with both the CFO and program directors.

## What Changes When? The CMS Horizon

I monitor three major shifts:

**1. Performance-Based Reimbursement (2024-2026):**
- CMS is testing models where a portion of DGME/IME ties to graduate outcomes: where they practice, who they serve, board pass rates.
- Initial pilots reward programs that produce residents who practice in shortage areas and primary care.
- Our internal medicine rural track is positioned for this. Our psychiatry expansion will compete for it.
- In 3-5 years, this could shift 10-15% of total GME revenue. That's significant.

**2. Off-Site Training and Community-Based Residencies:**
- CMS now allows more flexibility in where residents train. Community health centers, rural hospitals, and telemed platforms count as training venues if supervised and documented.
- This is important because it unlocks training without building new hospital capacity. DGME follows the resident, not the building.
- We've piloted a rural family medicine track using community hospitals in central Louisiana. Residents train with practicing community physicians, supervised by our faculty. The financial model works.
- As more programs adopt this, it will reshape where training happens and what health systems can afford to run.

**3. Consolidation and Specialty Redistribution:**
- Congress continues debating GME funding reforms. The direction is consistent: tie funding to workforce need, cap low-value specialties, incentivize primary care and shortage areas.
- Louisiana's position is favorable. We have documented shortages in family medicine, psychiatry, internal medicine, and OB/GYN. We don't have surpluses in most specialties.
- If redistribution happens, we could grow. But only if we can demonstrate need and capacity. That's the data I maintain.

## The Bottom Line

I work at the nexus of three realities: CMS pays for GME based on historical costs and resident counts, but those rules are shifting. Health systems compete for training slots and funding. And the physicians we train need to match the jobs that exist in Louisiana.

Most GME leaders pick one focus: financial optimization, educational quality, or workforce alignment. The institutions that win do all three simultaneously. That requires someone who can speak all three languages and translate between the CFO, the program directors, and state workforce planners.

That's the work I do.
