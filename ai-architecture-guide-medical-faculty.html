Module 02

Neural Network Architectures

The key point for faculty: architecture choice is not a technical footnote. It is a clinical and educational fit question.

General

Feedforward Networks

Data flows one direction from input to output. Useful for fixed-feature prediction tasks such as risk scores when temporal context is not central.

Imaging

Convolutional Neural Networks

CNNs are designed for image-like grid data. They detect local spatial patterns, making them appropriate for radiology, dermatology, pathology, and segmentation tasks.

Sequential

Recurrent Neural Networks

RNNs maintain hidden state across a sequence. They are suited to time-series problems such as ECG, ICU monitoring, and longitudinal clinical trajectories.

Dominant

Transformers

Transformers use attention rather than recurrence. They underpin LLMs and many text-heavy clinical AI tools, including summarization, synthesis, and question-answering systems.

Teaching implication: a faculty member who understands why CNNs fit imaging and transformers fit text is better prepared than one who can name several AI products but cannot evaluate task-architecture fit.

Module 03

Generative AI Systems

Generative AI should be taught as a set of architectures and failure modes, not as a list of chatbots.

High Impact

Large Language Models

Transformer-based systems trained on large text corpora. They can draft, summarize, explain, and reason across language, but can also produce fluent falsehoods.

RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Combines search over trusted sources with LLM generation. RAG helps ground outputs, expose citations, and update knowledge without retraining the base model.

Imaging

Diffusion Models

Generate images by learning to reverse a noise process. Useful for illustration and synthetic data, but anatomical plausibility must be independently validated.

Synthetic Data

GANs

Use a generator-discriminator competition to create synthetic examples. Potentially useful for privacy-preserving data and rare-disease augmentation, with instability risks.

Module 04

Curriculum Integration Frameworks

The design goal is durable AI literacy, not workshop-and-forget exposure.

P

Psychomotor

Hands-on skills: tool operation, annotation, prompting, output inspection, and workflow use.

A

Affective

Trust calibration: knowing when to rely, when to verify, and when to discount model confidence.

C

Cognitive

Conceptual knowledge: architecture, data, evaluation, limitations, and failure modes.

E

Embedded

Longitudinal integration across training rather than isolated short courses.

AI-PACE premise: medical AI curriculum should combine hands-on skill, trust calibration, conceptual architecture, and longitudinal embedding. Tool familiarity without architectural comprehension is fragile.
